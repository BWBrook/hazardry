# Custodian Craft

The Almanac tells you what a Custodian does. This chapter shows you what it looks like on a real evening, with real people, snacks going stale and someone’s phone face down by the dice.

We’ll follow four tables:

- **William** runs Service Duct Blues for three friends from work on Thursday nights, and he has been at it for a couple of months.
- **Alex** has run Candlelight Dungeons for years and still gets things wrong, which is part of why her players trust her.
- **Kat** runs the core game, a salvage crew picking over the Glass at Ferrous Gap.
- **Eddy** is running his first ever game, Twilight of the Northlands, for his sister and two of her friends.

You’ll see moments that sing and moments that fall flat. The flat ones are worth as much. Every Custodian has them, and the good ones notice.

## Start in the middle

William’s first session opens like this. Nobody has a drink in hand yet when he says:

“A coolant pulse slams down the crawl-tube. Behind a half-sealed hatch there’s a petty officer slumped against the wall, and the hatch has a red counter on it: ninety seconds. The next pulse is thirty seconds out. You could force the hatch, a Muscle test, but if it fails the pulse catches you. You could try to talk him awake through the gap. Or there’s the override fob on your belt. It works every time, but it’s logged, and that’s a point of Stress whatever happens. Or something else. Who’s moving?”

Sam is already leaning forward. Dee says “fob” before William has finished, then stops herself: “No, wait, that’s Stress.” David is reaching for the dice. There’s no small talk to get through and no shopping list. The game has started, and everyone knows what’s at stake and how long they have.

Compare William’s second campaign, a few months later, which opens with the crew in a station bar comparing gear. It’s pleasant enough. Two hours in, the table has made one roll. Nobody is bored exactly, but nobody is leaning forward either. Next session she opens on an alarm, and the difference is immediate.

A good opening doesn’t need a fight. It needs something already happening, something the characters care about, and a reason to act now. If the players finished last week by agreeing a careful plan, open in the middle of the plan going slightly wrong. Make sure the urgency is real, though. Alex once opened on a shout of “pin the brace!” from the dark, and it turned out to be some salvagers going about their work, in no danger at all. His players remembered that. The next time somebody shouted, they finished their snacks first.

## Say what’s at stake before the dice

Three sessions in, William’s crew have proof that the engineering chief has been covering up a scalding accident. In a corridor outside sickbay, the chief catches Imre’s sleeve. Sam plays Imre.

“He keeps his voice low,” says William. “‘This ends three careers, including mine. Give me until morning. Keep the audit trail to yourselves tonight, and I’ll put you both on the Tessaly roster.’ That’s the posting you’ve wanted all campaign, Sam. So here’s where you stand. You can take the deal. It’s a deal struck in a corridor and kept from the Captain, so that’s a point of Stress, whether it works out or not. You can say no, with no roll, and he goes cold on you. Or you can push back: offer to stand beside him while he tells the Captain himself, tonight. That’s a Harmony test. If it works, he agrees to report within the hour. If it fails, he gets to the Captain first with his own version, and blames you. There’s nothing hidden in that third one, so it costs no Stress.”

Sam looks at Dee, who shrugs: your call. David mutters, “Don’t take the roster for my sake.”

“The third one,” Sam says. “I’ll walk him there myself.”

He rolls a 6 under his 12. The chief’s hand stops shaking. “Within the hour,” he says. “In my own words.”

What William did there is the most important habit in this chapter. Before anyone touched a die, Sam knew what each choice would cost and what it might win. So when he chose, he chose for real, and when the dice landed, nobody argued.

Here’s the same moment done badly, at Eddy’s table. His sister Hannah wants her character to cross a frozen ford. Eddy says, “Roll Nimbleness.” She rolls a 17 and misses. Eddy thinks for a moment, then says, “You go through the ice. That’s 2 damage, and you lose your pack.”

“You didn’t say I could lose the pack!”

She’s right. The pack might be a fine consequence, but she had no chance to weigh it. She might have taken her pack off first, or crossed somewhere else. Stakes that turn up after the dice feel like a trap, however fair they are. Say them first, briefly, as part of the offer: what success gets, and what failure costs.

One more refinement. When you name a cost, say whether it comes from the roll itself or from refusing an offer. “If you fail, the pulse catches you” is different from “if you turn him down, he goes cold”. Players need to know whether a cleverer approach could still save them.

## Not everything needs a roll

Once the hatch on William’s ship is open and the pulse has passed, Dee asks, “Do I need to roll to drag him out?”

“No,” says William. “Nobody’s racing the seal any more. You haul him out, and he’s heavy, and he smells of coolant.”

That’s the right call, and it’s quick. A roll is for a moment that’s uncertain and matters. When the danger has passed, or failure would just mean trying again, let the character do it. In Alex’s dungeon, Ade wants to copy an inscription from a ledge using a mirror and a lantern. It takes a turn and no roll, because nothing bad happens if he gets a letter wrong. A friendly witness will tell you what she saw without anyone throwing dice.

The trap at the other end is generosity. A finale that resolves without a roll can be exactly right, if the players have gathered so much evidence that there’s no real doubt left. Just check you aren’t sparing them a risk the story promised them.

## Let the players choose

This is where new Custodians stumble most, usually by trying to be helpful.

Alex’s party is at an underground sluice. Water is pouring through, and they need to pull a lever on the far side of the channel to close it. Kit’s character, Mara, wants to wade across tied to a rope. Jo’s character, Sera, wants to throw a loop of rope over the lever from where they stand. Both say so at once.

“Both of those could work,” says Alex, “and they’d each have their own risks. Mara, wading is a Strength test, and if it fails you’re dragged back to the landing, unhurt but soaked. Sera, the throw is Dexterity, and if you miss the rope fouls on the grate and costs you a turn to free. But there’s one rope. It can’t be a tether and a lasso at the same moment. So who goes first? You two can agree, or pick another plan, or if you both like, I’ll roll a die between you. And the water’s not rising while you sort it out.”

Kit laughs. “Go on, Jo, have your lasso.” Sera makes her throw, and nobody feels hard done by.

Now Eddy, the same week, with three players who all want to guide the party through a fen. He looks at their sheets, sees that Corwen has the best Wisdom, and says, “Two of you said Corwen, so Corwen leads.” It’s quick. But Hannah never said Corwen, and she spends the rest of the scene a little quieter. When choices collide, say so and let the players settle it. Never quietly pick the roller yourself, by the best odds or by a show of hands nobody asked for.

The same instinct shows up with Luck. Later that night, Hannah’s character Merewen tries to talk a suspicious watchman round. She needs 11 and rolls 18. She has 10 Hope tokens. Eddy thinks, seven tokens is far too many, she won’t want that, and moves on: “He doesn’t believe you.” Then, because the scene feels stuck, he lets Corwen try the same argument and roll again. Corwen succeeds.

Afterwards Hannah says, gently, “I’d have paid the seven, you know.” And the second roll took away her failure, because it was the same argument from a different mouth. Here’s the fix:

- **Offer every spend that changes the result, however expensive,** and give the exact price. Whether it’s worth it is the player’s call.
- **Agree who’s speaking before the roll.** Once that roll is made, it stands. Someone else gets a go only if something has really changed: new leverage, a new approach, a new moment.

Two smaller courtesies round this out:

- **If the players think better of an action before it happens, it didn’t happen.** Eddy’s players start to lean on a nervous innkeeper, then decide against it. Later Eddy realises he’d quietly moved his villain’s plans along because of the leaning that never happened. Time spent is spent, but the action’s consequences don’t land.
- **Never speak for a player’s character.** Not even “she says no”. Ask.

## The world moves when you’re not looking

On Kat’s table, the salvage crew have refused an offer from a rival boss’s outrider. The outrider rides back up the ridge.

“Within a few minutes,” says Kat, “the mule bells along the ridge start up faster. A rider peels off the skyline and disappears behind it.”

Ollie says, “That’s not good, is it?”

“You tell me.”

An hour of game time later, a dust devil tears across the flats. In its wake the crew can see two plumes of dust on the ridge, running ahead of them towards the cellar they’re making for. The bells are much nearer.

Kat has a clock in her notes, counting down to when the rival crew reaches the cellar. She never shows it. The players never learn its number, but they know exactly what it means, and the table goes quiet in a good way. Signs do work that numbers can’t. Mule bells, a rider on the skyline, a lantern sputtering, stone scraping somewhere below: give the players those, and they’ll feel the clock without seeing it.

Eddy learns this the hard way. Mid-session he says, “The Watch has reached its fourth sign,” and his players immediately start doing arithmetic instead of worrying. If you hear yourself say a number for something hidden, describe the fourth sign instead.

One more thing about clocks. If one is about to fill, make sure the players have had at least one real chance to do something about it. William once let a hidden clock run out before her crew had any way to interrupt it. Nothing was wrong with the clock. It just felt like a rug pulled out.

## Pressure is a fuse, not a fine

By his third session, Eddy has found his feet. His company comes down into a drowned barrow where the dead lie in rows along the steps.

“Looking at them properly,” he says, “will get under your skin. That’s a point of Dread for anyone who looks. And the singing starts again at the next bell.”

Hannah grins. “Merewen stops her ears and keeps her eyes on the stairs.”

She pays nothing, and that’s fine. Eddy told them the price before they chose, and they found a way round. That’s the system working.

But watch your players over a few sessions, and you may notice they get very good at never paying Pressure. They camp instead of pressing on. They spend Luck instead of taking Pressure, and take the long way every time it’s free. If the fuse never burns, look at your situation before you blame the dice. When the only way forward has a price on it, and the players get to choose how they pay, Pressure does its job: the tension rises because they chose to push.

**When the fuse reaches 5, something has to change.** On Kat’s table, the crew are out in the open as the hour ends, with the rivals still in sight. That’s a point of Pressure, and it tips the track to 5.

“A dust devil hits the lead walker first,” Kat says. “Ollie, that’s Cass. It tears the coiled rope out of your hands and flings it away across the Glass. Grit scours your eyes. Until you can rinse them out at a rest, anything that depends on seeing is at Disadvantage. Pressure goes back to 0.”

Ollie groans and laughs at once, which is the right reaction. The crisis has a target, a reason (Cass was out in front), and a consequence that will matter for the rest of the night.

Alex, on a tired evening, once narrated a crisis as a character “stumbling, but no harm done”. Everyone noticed. A crisis that changes nothing teaches the table that 5 is nothing to fear.

Describe a crisis in words that match what it does. Alex’s cramp crisis left a character unable to run. He described it as her hand seizing around a rope, and both players assumed she’d fumble her next climb. Say what the effect is, plainly, once the colour is done.

## People want things

Kat’s rival boss is called Redd. He wants a fair price, not a fight. He is slow and courteous, and he has a proverb for everything. When Femi’s character offers eight doses of medicine for a disputed salvage claim, Redd hooks his thumbs in his belt and says:

“Proverb says: the jar that cracks first is the one nobody oiled. Eight doses for a paper is a kind offer, friend. Not kind enough.”

Femi laughs out loud. Then he starts thinking about what Redd might actually take. Redd’s manner has told the table what currency he deals in.

Give each important character three things: what they want, how they come across, and one line they won’t cross. Keep the line to yourself, and let the players find it by watching. In Alex’s game, a salvager who came for a bronze bell’s copper refuses to pull its chain while anyone is beneath it. “I’m not doing that with someone under it,” she says flatly, and the standoff becomes a negotiation.

In another of his dungeons, a ghoul hunched over a broken coffin wants only tales of grief. Wen, the quietest player at the table, kneels her character down and tells it, in her own words, about a saint who sat in the barrow every night counting the names of the dead, and died with one name still missing. The table goes silent. Alex doesn’t ask for a roll. The ghoul thanks her and steps aside. People remember that one for years.

The easiest place to slip with characters is the helpful recap. Kat, summing up a long scene, says, “And Wren will never take more than she’s owed.” Wren never said that. She’d said her father’s share should come to her, which is a different thing. Later, Grace builds a plan on Kat’s line, and Kat has to unpick it.

When you summarise, say what characters said or did, and keep your private notes out of it: “Wren says the forty percent is hers.”

The same goes for enemies. “He’s bleeding from the knee and the thigh now, and his bellow has thinned” tells the players he’s in trouble. “Any hit will drop him” tells them exactly how much Stamina he has left. One of those keeps the fight alive.

## Be honest about danger

Alex’s party finds a chest with a thin black slot beside the lock. Ade asks if he can look closer.

“With your mirror you can see a needle in the slot,” says Alex. “Sprung, waiting. If you lift the lid by hand, your fingers are right in its path.”

Ade jams a spike over the spring and opens the chest without a roll. The danger was real, it could be read, and it could be beaten. The players feel clever, which is the whole point.

Later the same night, they want to lower a heavy bell safely, and Alex says it can’t be done without some independent support, “and none’s rigged yet”. His notes actually allowed a couple of ways to make it safe, and the players took his “can’t” as final. When an approach is hard, say what makes it hard and what it would take. A flat no sounds more final than you mean it.

A few more habits:

- **Once you’ve offered an option, keep it,** unless something in the story changes, and then say what changed. Kat once offered “all three of you can go to him”, then a few minutes later allowed only two. Grace noticed.
- **When the players are short of something, mention what could help.** The party’s lantern died in Alex’s dungeon, and nobody remembered that Ade’s character knew a light cantrip, Alex included.
- **Say whether help helps before the roll.** If one character holds the lantern while another works a lock, say up front whether that gives Advantage, and why. Alex once rolled first, then said “no Advantage for the light”. On those dice, Advantage would have meant success. Instead his player paid two coins of Fortune to scrape through. Nothing is worse than finding out the terms after you’ve committed.

Get the numbers right before the choice, too. On Eddy’s first night, Raff’s character Hild lands a big axe blow. Eddy says it does 2 damage, and that one more token would make it 3. Raff decides to save his tokens. Ten minutes later Eddy realises the blow already did 3, and that finishing the creature off would have been cheap. He owns it straight away, which is good, but Raff made a choice on wrong numbers, and a correction can’t hand that choice back. Slow down, check, then offer.

## When you get it wrong

You will. Kat, deep in a tense bargain, forgets to give Femi the Advantage she’d agreed for his ledger and scales. She only notices after the dice, and the dice say Redd wins.

“Hang on,” she says. “That’s my mistake. You should have had Advantage there, and I rolled without it. The dice are already out, so here’s where we stand, and here are your choices.”

That’s exactly right: say so, keep the dice, and carry on with what the rules allow. Then, because she feels bad, she adds, “Tell you what, if you spend four Luck to win it, I’ll give you two back.” Femi takes the deal happily. It’s kind. But there’s no such rule, and now the table has learned that the Custodian hands back tokens when she feels guilty. Next time, Kat leaves it at the apology.

A minute later Femi tries to change what winning the bargain would get him. Kat holds the line: “The stakes were set before we rolled. You can choose not to spend, but you can’t rewrite what the win buys.” He grumbles, keeps his tokens, and tries a completely fresh approach. That’s the honest version.

And when a scene’s question has been answered, stop rolling. Alex’s party carries the reliquary out through the portcullis, which is what the whole scene was about, and then Jo asks to roll to hold the gate a little longer for the others. They’re already through. The roll can’t change anything, so let the scene end.

## Ending the scene, ending the night

A scene ends when its question is answered, dropped or changed. If the party spends twenty minutes bracing a door against rising water, that’s one scene, however many rolls it takes. It doesn’t become three just because you’d like a milestone sooner.

Mark a scene as perilous when failing its big choice could cost a character Stamina, cost someone’s life, or cost the goal. Some cases catch new Custodians out:

- A race against time to fix something is perilous, even if they fix it.
- A scene where a stranger’s life hangs on what the party does is perilous, even if nobody rolls.
- A clever safeguard the players earn halfway through doesn’t make the danger they faced disappear.

A milestone comes after three or four perilous scenes. When one arrives, say what it refills and what it doesn’t. It isn’t a rest, so a crisis that’s still hanging over a character stays put.

At the end of the night, close the books the way Alex does. He runs through what each character spent, lost or left behind, and each character’s Stamina against its maximum. He names the threads still loose. Then he goes round the table and gives everyone a last line in character. Kit’s is usually a joke. Wen’s never is. Then they argue about whose turn it is to bring snacks.

## If you remember nothing else

- Start in the middle of something.
- Say what’s at stake before the dice.
- Let the players choose: who acts, what to spend, when to stop.
- Show the world moving through signs, not numbers.
- Make Pressure bite when it fills.
- Give people wants, and keep their secrets.
- Be honest about danger, and honest about your mistakes.
