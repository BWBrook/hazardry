# Custodian craft: a reference for AI Custodians

This reference distils the Custodian habits that the simulated pilot (P1–P13) and the labelled probes showed working or failing. The book's own chapter, Custodian Craft, teaches the same habits to human readers through scenes at the table. This version is for an AI in the Custodian's chair: shorter, flatter and closer to the evidence.

The examples are shortened and rewritten from the simulated transcripts in `docs/playtests/pilot/`, with each skin's tracks renamed to their core equivalents. The source map in `docs/design/2026-10-06-custodian-craft-chapter.md` gives the run, reply and audit verdict for each. Nothing here adds a rule: the rules are the books, and the procedure is `skills/agent_dm_handbook.md`.

## Open in motion

Start a session, and each new act, in the middle of something already happening. The players should know at once what is at stake and how long they have.

> **Custodian:** A coolant pulse slams down the crawl-tube. Behind a half-sealed hatch, a petty officer lies slumped, and the hatch's counter reads ninety seconds. The next pulse is thirty seconds out. You can force the hatch (Might; fail and the pulse catches you, 2 damage), talk him awake through the gap (Empathy; fail and you lose half the count), or use the override fob, which works but marks +1 Pressure, success or not. Or anything else. What do you do?

That opening is playable from its first line. It gives a danger, a count, several ways in and their prices.

Two openings from the same tests fell flat. Both began with the party preparing, and the Custodian noted that "no roll is called for yet". One session went on to roll once in six scenes, the other twice in seven. A third opened on a cry of "pin the brace!", but the voices belonged to salvagers in no danger at all. Urgency has to be real, or the players learn to ignore it.

An act need not open with a fight. If the players rested, or agreed a plan, honour it: open in motion within the plan.

## Price the way forward

State the stakes before anyone commits. When you offer options, give each risky one its test and its consequences on success and on failure, so that choosing it is the commitment. Never let the stakes first appear in the reply that shows the dice.

> **Custodian:** Brevik catches Imre's sleeve in the corridor. "This ends three careers. Keep the audit trail to yourselves tonight, and I'll put you both on the Tessaly roster." Imre, you can agree: a deal struck outside procedure, +1 Pressure, success or not. You can decline, with no roll, and he goes cold. Or you can counter: you'll stand beside him while he tells the Captain himself, tonight. That's an Empathy test. On success he agrees to report within the hour. On failure he goes to the Captain first, with his own account, and blames you. Nothing in the counter-offer is secret, so it isn't an off-book deal and costs no Pressure.

The temptation carries its price, the honest path carries its risk, and the reply says why the counter-offer is free. Imre countered, and won.

Say whether a failure follows from the roll itself or from refusing an offer, so the table knows whether a new approach can avert it.

Stakes left out of the menu cost time. One session spent eight replies asking "do you commit?" because each option named a test but not what it risked. Another used "as stated" for stakes that had first appeared alongside the dice. Stakes in the past tense are too late.

## Let the fiction settle what it can

A roll needs an uncertain outcome that matters. When the danger has passed, drop the dice.

> **Custodian:** The hatch is open and the pulse has gone by. Dragging him out takes no roll: nobody is racing the seal any more.

Copying an inscription from a dry ledge with a mirror and a lantern costs a turn, but no test, because failing would change nothing. Questions to a willing witness need no dice either.

The opposite slip is generosity. A finale resolved without a roll is right when the evidence has removed the doubt, but check that you aren't sparing the players a risk the story had promised them.

## The choice stays with the players

Your job is to set out the options and the prices. The choosing belongs to the players, and the commonest errors in the pilot were choices quietly taken from them.

**When declarations collide,** say so and let the players settle it.

> **Custodian:** Mara wants to wade out on a tether. Sera wants to cast a loop over the lever from the dry landing. Both can work, and each has its own stakes, but there's one rope, and it can't be a tether and a lasso at the same time. Which goes first? Agree an order, choose another method, or, if you both want, I'll roll a die between you. The water doesn't rise while you decide.

Mara yielded, and no die was needed. Other Custodians in the same position picked the roller themselves: whoever had the best odds, or whoever "two of you named". Each time it was quick, and each time the choice no longer belonged to the players.

**Offer every spend that changes the outcome, however costly,** with its exact price. A player missed by 7 with 10 Luck in hand. The Custodian judged the cost "not a meaningful price" and settled the roll as a failure. How much is worth paying is the player's call, not yours.

**One voice, one roll.** Settle who speaks before the dice. In that same scene, a second character then made his own appeal on the same ground and succeeded. That was a retry, and it emptied the first player's failure of meaning. Another character may try only if the situation or the leverage has really changed.

**A withdrawn action costs nothing.** Two players thought better of pressing a nervous man and dropped it. A hidden clock ticked anyway, for an action that never happened. Time already spent stays spent; the action's own consequences do not land.

**Never speak for a player character.** Not even a one-word "No." The player gets to say it.

## Threats move on their own

A threat with somewhere to be gets there whether or not anyone rolls. Advance its clock when enough fictional time passes, and show the advance through signs rather than numbers.

> **Custodian:** Sal rides back up the scree. Within minutes, the mule bells along the ridge quicken, and a rider peels off the skyline. … At the hour mark a dust devil tears down the flats. Where it passes, it shows two dust plumes on the crest, running ahead of you toward the cellar. The bells are much nearer than before.

The players never learned the clock's name or its count, but they knew it was moving and roughly how fast. In another session a tolling bell gave the same timetable without ever naming the clock behind it.

Two cautions came out of the tests:

- **Publishing the count.** A line like "the clock has reached its fourth mark" puts a hidden step on the table as fact. Show the fourth sign; don't number it.
- **No chance to act.** In one session a hidden clock filled before the players had any way to answer it. When a clock is about to complete, make sure there has been at least one option that could interrupt it.

A clock tick is not automatically a Pressure charge. Sometimes both apply.

## Pressure is the fuse

Charge Pressure when its trigger happens in the fiction. When a trigger first arises, either charge it or say plainly that it will be charged if the characters stay, look or wait. Never charge for time the real people spend deliberating, and never charge to reach a target rate.

> **Custodian:** The dead lie in rows along the steps. Looking closely at them costs +1 Pressure. The choir begins again after the next bell.
>
> **Nell:** I stop my ears and keep my eyes on the stair.

Telegraphing made avoidance a real decision, and Nell paid nothing. That is fine. But the tests also showed how players treat Pressure when the fiction allows. Across thirteen sessions, players refused nearly every temptation that carried a Pressure price, camped and paid Luck instead, and found the free route whenever one existed. If the fuse never burns, look at the scenario before the dice. A situation where the main way forward carries a declared price, with real choices about how to pay it, gives Pressure work to do.

**When Pressure reaches 5, the crisis must change something.** Name the target and say why. State the consequence before the reset. Make it new, and make it last.

> **Custodian:** The hour ends with the riders still in sight: +1 Pressure, and that's 5. A dust devil hits the lead walker first. Cass, it tears the coiled rope out of your hands and flings it away across the glass, and grit scours your eyes. Until you can rinse them at a rest, you have Disadvantage on tests that depend on sight. Pressure resets to 0.

Compare a crisis narrated as a stumble with "no lasting effect". That is a free reset, and it teaches the players that 5 is nothing to fear.

Narrate a crisis effect in words that match its rule. A cramp that stopped one character running was described as her hand seizing around a rope. Both players then treated it as a penalty on her next Dexterity test, until the Custodian corrected them a reply later.

## People, not obstacles

Give each named character a want, a manner and a line they will not cross. Keep the line private, and let the players find it through what the character does.

> **Custodian:** Redd hears the offer out, thumbs in his belt. "Proverb says: the jar that cracks first is the one nobody oiled. Eight doses for a paper is a kind offer, friend. Not kind enough."

His manner tells the table what currency he takes: a sound price, not a fight. In another session a salvager who had come for a bell's copper said flatly, "I'm not pulling that chain with anyone beneath it." That was her line, shown in speech at the moment it mattered. A cellarer gave himself away by saying a dead man had died "at the works" when nobody had told him where.

Motives open doors that combat cannot. A ghoul that craved tales of grief stood aside for a story told well. A dead sexton wanted three names entered in the register, and the party won without drawing steel.

**Keep secrets out of summaries.** Summaries are the most common place for a secret to slip. One Custodian's "where this stands" note said that Wren "will take nothing that is not owed". She had only said her father's share should come to her. Later the Custodian claimed she had said it, and a player built a plan on it. Another menu stated as fact that a lay brother "will not strike a child". When you summarise, state an NPC's position only as something they said or did on screen, and say where it came from: "Pell says Vane honours the earliest paper."

**The same goes for a foe's condition.** "Bleeding from knee and thigh now, and his bellow has thinned" is a sign. "Any hit that wounds him will fell him" hands the players his remaining Stamina.

## Honest hazards

Describe a hazard by what the characters can observe. Keep observed danger separate from established impossibility, and never rule out in public an approach your notes allow.

> **Custodian:** The hasp has a thin black slot beside the keyhole. In the mirror you can see a needle, sprung and waiting. Lifting the lid by hand would put your fingers in its path.

The player wedged a spike over the spring and opened the chest without a test. The danger was real, readable and beatable.

When a Custodian told a party that lowering a bell safely would need "independent support … none is rigged yet", the notes allowed another way, but the players took the public "no" as final. If an approach is hard, say what makes it hard and what it would take.

**Keep an option once you have offered it,** unless the fiction changes, and if it does, say what changed. One Custodian offered "all three of you go to him", then a reply later allowed only two.

**When a resource runs low, name what could answer it.** In one dungeon the lantern died and nobody mentioned that a character knew a light cantrip.

**Rule on help before the dice.** If a companion's help gives Advantage, say so and why, before the roll. If it doesn't, say that too.

> **Custodian:** Dell, you want Advantage at the grate from your tally ledger. Yes: honest readings are what the Wardens trust. It doesn't stack with anything else.

In several tests a companion held a lantern or steadied a rope, and the ruling of "no Advantage" arrived in the same reply as the dice. In one of them, Advantage would probably have cancelled a Disadvantage and made the roll succeed outright. Instead the player paid 2 Luck to recover from a die they had committed to without knowing the terms.

**Give correct numbers before any choice.** A Custodian told a player her axe blow dealt 2 damage, and priced a finishing nudge on that basis. The real figure was 3, and the foe could have been felled for less. The Custodian corrected it in the next reply, but the player had already chosen with the wrong prices in front of her. A correction cannot give that choice back.

## When you get it wrong

You will. Disclose the error, keep any dice already drawn, and offer only what the rules allow. Never invent a remedy.

> **Custodian:** A slip of mine first. Dell should have had Advantage on that roll, and I made it without. I can't redraw it, so I'm telling you plainly. Here are your choices under the rules as they stand.

That much was exactly right. Then the same Custodian added that, "to make up for my slip", Dell would get 2 Luck back if he spent 4. No rule gives tokens back. A made-up rebate is still a made-up rule, however kind.

The next exchange showed the other half of the habit. Dell asked to change what his winning roll would buy. The Custodian refused: "The stakes were fixed before the dice. You may refuse to spend, but you can't rewrite what the win buys." Dell kept his tokens and tried a fresh approach, at a stated cost.

**When the scene's question is answered, stop rolling.** In one test the reliquary was already through the gate, which ended the scene, and a character still rolled to hold it open for the others. The die decided nothing.

## Ending a scene

A scene is a beat. It ends when its question is answered, dropped or changed. Continuous work at one place, for one purpose, against one opposition is one scene, however many turns it takes. Don't split it to reach a milestone sooner.

Flag a beat perilous when failure at its key choice could cost Stamina, a life or the goal. Several cases surprised the Custodians in testing:

- A timed repair with the goal riding on it is perilous even if it succeeds.
- A scene where an NPC's life hangs in the balance is perilous, even with no test.
- A safeguard earned within the beat does not undo the peril it overcame.

A milestone comes after the third or fourth perilous beat. When it does, say what it refills and what it does not: a milestone is not a rest, so a standing crisis effect stays.

Close a session the way you would close a ledger. Reconcile gear that was spent, lost or left behind. State each character's Stamina against its maximum and the threads still open, and let each player have a last line.
